# Make sonic decisions audible

Start with the job: a listener should understand a next action, recognize a standalone sonic logo, remember a launch name, or feel the end of a story. Choose a hierarchy before choosing sounds. For a narrated product explainer, speech usually occupies the foreground; music establishes continuity; effects confirm meaningful action. For a silent autoplay placement, captions and visuals carry meaning, and sound becomes an enhancement. Do not promise an audio cue will communicate essential information to everyone.

## Translate a brief into a palette

Write decisions a producer can execute and a reviewer can reject:

| Vague request | Useful production instruction | Review question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calm | Sparse soft plucked notes; no busy percussion under the opening sentence; two-beat rest before the product name | Does the first listen preserve every word? |
| Precise | Short dry confirmation tick only when the final state lands, with no extra click on intermediate motion | Does the tick identify completion rather than every cursor movement? |
| Warm voice | Conversational delivery at the speaker's natural pace, relaxed phrase ends, no sales-announcer projection | Does it sound like a person explaining a useful change? |
| Confident finish | Music resolves after the last spoken consonant; short tail stays inside the final readable hold | Can the name and call to action be recalled? |

Record tonal register, density, rhythmic pulse, attack/decay, spatial width, foreground/background role and contrast. A sonic motif can be a two-note interval or rhythmic shape; avoid suggesting that a random generated melody is a legally cleared brand identity. Let brand direction constrain sound without changing its existing name, colors or claims.

## Build a sound map

1. Read the approved brief and identify audible semantic events: problem, reveal, action, confirmation, name and CTA where included. For coordinated film, also watch/read the approved storyboard; standalone sound needs no storyboard.
2. Assign a sound function to each: orient, anticipate, connect, confirm, resolve, or deliberate silence. Remove events with no function.
3. Reserve room around speech onsets and endings. A transient exactly on a crucial consonant can mask it even when the music average is low.
4. Mark changes against the approved cue/timebase record. Motion owns coordinated film frames; Sound owns Lead-approved standalone timing. For film, a musical bar need not force a cut, and an accent can follow a visual landing without moving approved timing.
5. Compare two bounded treatments with a reason, such as sparse plucks versus low sustained texture. Do not generate a pile of candidates before the sonic vocabulary is agreed.

Use headphones and a small speaker at comfortable matched playback levels for review. Check stereo and mono; avoid a wide-only cue that disappears when summed. A direction document can request these checks, but must not claim anyone listened until a named reviewer records timecoded findings.

## Revision discipline

Identify the disagreement precisely: voice too urgent, music masking a word, an effect mistimed, or a brand cue too playful. Change one dimension and preserve the approved script, timing and route. Limit the next review to the affected passage plus its transitions, then recheck the whole final export. A change to total length or cue boundaries returns through Chris to Motion for coordinated film timing or Sound for standalone timing approval; Kaan owns exact speech wording. Reissue the same canonical timeline when helpers need it, calculating a new fingerprint from changed content rather than copying the old file.

Anti-patterns: loudness as a proxy for energy; a whoosh on every transition; filling silence by default; referencing a familiar artist's exact performance; approving a treatment from a waveform image. Evidence is the brief version, decision owner, frame cue proposal and later listening notes, not the author's confidence.
